Principle 01

Expected value over outcomes

Good decisions can produce bad results. Bad decisions can get lucky. Judge the process, not the result. "Resulting" — mistaking a bad outcome for a bad decision — is the #1 enemy of disciplined thinking.

Decision Science

Mitigating the Common Traps

1 Confirmation Bias

What it is: Favoring information that supports your existing belief

Why it's dangerous: Leads to blind spots and overconfidence

The Fix

Force disconfirming evidence. Assign someone to argue the opposite case.

"What would have to be true for this to be wrong?"
2 Anchoring Bias

What it is: Overweighting the first piece of information — initial estimate, first opinion

Why it's dangerous: Distorts judgment and narrows thinking

The Fix

Generate independent inputs first. Have team members write down estimates before any discussion begins.

3 Overconfidence Bias

What it is: Overestimating certainty or accuracy of your judgment

Why it's dangerous: Underestimates risk and ignores contingencies

The Fix

Use probability ranges instead of confident predictions.

"What's the % likelihood — and what would change it?"
4 Loss Aversion

What it is: Avoiding losses more strongly than pursuing equivalent gains

Why it's dangerous: Leads to risk avoidance and missed opportunities

The Fix — Reframe Both Ways

Ask both: "What do we lose if we act?" and "What do we lose if we don't act?"

5 Sunk Cost Fallacy

What it is: Continuing a path because of past investment — time, money, or ego

Why it's dangerous: Keeps teams stuck in bad decisions long after the evidence turns

The Fix — "Today Decision" Test

Reset the frame entirely.

"If we weren't already in this, would we choose it now?"

Shorthand for Avoiding Traps

Confirmation → Challenge assumptions
Anchoring → Separate inputs
Overconfidence → Quantify uncertainty
Loss Aversion → Reframe tradeoffs
Sunk Cost → Restart the decision

Every decision is a bet. Play like a disciplined pro.
